@ sixsisters | The Six Shears of the Weaver Sisters
> In the wool town of Fleecemarket there were six sisters who ran the great weaving hall, and each had her own pair of shears, and each pair was famous for something. When the hall burned down, the six pairs were all that came out of the fire. The sisters are long gone. The shears are still in the town, passed down, one family each.
shears | The Eldest's Shears | Agatha, the eldest, cut the first fleece of every season, and nobody else was allowed to, not even the mayor. She said the first cut decides the year. The year she was ill and her sister cut it instead, the wool came in thin and the river flooded. Nobody let her be ill again.
shears | The Second's Shears | Brigid cut cloth for the poor, for free, at the back door of the hall, at night, so her sisters wouldn't know. They knew. They left extra cloth by the back door every evening and never said a word. Brigid thought she had a secret for forty years. It was the sisters' favourite secret.
shears | The Third's Shears | Cressida's shears were so sharp she could cut a falling feather in half in the air. She did it at the town fair every year. She said it was a trick of the wrist. It was not. It was forty years of practice and a very good whetstone. The whetstone is in the town museum.
shears | The Fourth's Shears | Della cut the patterns, and her patterns were copied in every town in the kingdom. She never sold one. She said a pattern is like a song: it belongs to whoever hums it. The guild of tailors tried to make her charge. She cut their charter into the shape of a duck.
shears | The Fifth's Shears | Esme never cut anything at all. She held her shears at every wedding, birth and funeral in the town, and said a few words, and handed them to whoever needed to cut a ribbon or a cord or a lock of hair. She was the town's ceremony. When she died, the whole town came.
shears | The Youngest's Shears | Fern carried all six pairs out of the burning hall in her apron, the night of the fire, with her hair alight. She said she didn't think. She'd grabbed what mattered. She lived to ninety-seven. When she was asked, at the end, what she'd have grabbed instead, she said: "my sisters."

@ sevenrods | The Seven Rods of the Pier Club
> Every morning for fifty years, seven old men fished off the end of the pier at Saltwhistle, side by side, in the same order, from the same planks. They called themselves the Pier Club. They had rules. They had feuds. They had a membership waiting list longer than the pier. Now there's one left. He still brings every one of their rods.
fishing_rod | Albert's Rod | Albert founded the club, and made the rules, which were three: no talking before the first fish, no talking about the war, and no talking about Albert's wife, who had left him for the man who sold bait. Albert broke all three rules every day. Nobody ever mentioned it.
fishing_rod | Big Tom's Rod | Big Tom was the biggest man in the town and caught the smallest fish, every day, for fifty years. He measured them with a little ruler and wrote them in a little book. The book has eighteen thousand fish in it. None of them is longer than his hand.
fishing_rod | The Twins' Rod | The Halloway twins shared one rod and one place on the pier and took turns, a fish each, for fifty years. They never once argued about whose turn it was. They argued about everything else. When one died, the other kept fishing, a fish each. He threw every second one back.
fishing_rod | Doc's Rod | The town doctor fished with the club on his days off and refused, on the pier, to give any medical advice whatsoever. "Not on the pier," he'd say. When Albert had his heart attack on the end of the pier, the doctor said "not on the pier" and saved his life anyway.
fishing_rod | The Vicar's Rod | The vicar joined late, and fished badly, and prayed loudly for fish, and caught more than any of them. The others said it wasn't fair. The vicar said it was entirely fair, and that they were welcome to pray too. Big Tom tried. He caught a slightly bigger small fish.
fishing_rod | The New Boy's Rod | The new boy joined at sixty-one, after thirty years on the waiting list, and was the new boy for the next twenty-six years. He's the one who's left. He brings all their rods to the end of the pier every morning and sets them up side by side. He doesn't talk before the first fish.
fishing_rod | The Empty Place | There's a gap at the end of the line, where one more would stand, if there were one more. The club never had one. The last member says it was kept for whoever came next. Last week a girl of nine stood in the gap with her own rod. He didn't say anything. He passed her the bait.

@ fourspears | The Spear-Fisher's Year
> My husband fished the shallows with a spear for forty years, a different spear for every season, and when he died I took them down from the wall, all four, and went out into the water in his place, because the family had to eat and because I wanted to know what he had loved about it. This is my first year of it. I'm sixty-one.
iron_spear | Winter | Winter spear: iron, heavy, for the slow cold fish at the bottom of the bay. My hands went numb the first morning and I dropped it twice. The neighbours watched from the harbour wall and said nothing. On the third morning I caught a cod. I carried it home through the village with my head up.
copper_spear | Spring | Spring spear: copper, light, for the quick silver fish that come in with the warm water. I missed for a month. Then one morning the whole shoal turned at once and I struck without thinking, the way he always said you had to, and there it was on the point. I laughed out loud in the water.
stone_spear | Summer | Summer spear: stone, the oldest, his grandfather's, for the flatfish in the sand. You stand in the shallows and wait for the sand to breathe. I stood there for hours, every day, the sun on my neck. I think this was the part he loved. The waiting. I didn't understand that, until this summer.
diamond_spear | Autumn | Autumn spear: diamond, his one extravagance, for the great fish that come past the point before the storms. I caught one. Bigger than my grandson. The whole village came down to see. I gave it away, all of it, the way he would have. Next year I'll do it all again. I'm getting good.

@ brokenpicks | Seven Broken Picks of the Harrowdale Mine
> When the Harrowdale mine closed, the last foreman, a woman called Ruth Tamsworth, hung up on the wall of the old office every pickaxe that had broken in the mine in her forty years there, with a card for each one. There were hundreds. When the office was pulled down, seven were saved. These are those seven, and their cards.
iron_pickaxe | Card One | "Broken by Gus Arnley, day one of the mine, on the first stone he swung at. He said it was a sign. It was. It was a sign that he'd bought a cheap pickaxe. He mined here thirty years. He bought better pickaxes. He kept this one in his locker, as a lesson."
stone_pickaxe | Card Two | "Broken by a boy of eleven, who was not supposed to be down here, and who dug through to the old dwarf tunnels before anyone noticed he was gone. He found them. He found a way out of them too. He's the mine engineer now. Still not supposed to be down there half the time."
iron_pickaxe | Card Three | "Broken in the cave-in of the third winter, by Hal Grimby, who used it to dig six men out by himself over two days. It broke in his hands on the last man. He dug the last bit with his hands. Every one of them lived. Hal never mentioned it once. We mention it for him."
golden_pickaxe | Card Four | "Broken by the mine owner's son, who came down with a golden pickaxe to show off, and swung it at a rock, and it broke in one swing, because gold is soft. Everyone laughed. He laughed too. He worked down here for six months after that. He was all right, actually."
diamond_pickaxe | Card Five | "Broken by nobody: it was dropped down the main shaft by a careless clerk and landed point-first on the bottom. It was the only diamond pickaxe the mine ever owned. The clerk was fined a month's pay. He paid it gladly. He said it was cheaper than being down here with the men."
wooden_pickaxe | Card Six | "Broken by me. My first. The day my father brought me down here at fourteen. I swung it at the wall and it snapped and I cried. He said every miner's first pick breaks. He said it's how the mountain says hello. He's buried at the top of the hill now. I say hello to him."
iron_pickaxe | Card Seven | "Broken on the last day the mine was open, by the whole crew together, each of us taking one swing at the last rock, until it gave. It was symbolic. Also, it was a very hard rock. We went up and we didn't come back down. Forty years. That's that."

@ arrowswordsmith | The Swordsmith's Last Six Blades
> The swordsmith Orren Vale made swords for kings for fifty years, and swore he'd make no more when he turned seventy. On his seventieth birthday, six people came to his forge on the same morning, each needing a sword, each for a different reason. He made six more. He kept a note of who they were for.
iron_sword | For the Girl Who Wanted to Be a Knight | "Eleven years old. Her father said no. She came anyway. I made her a small blade, light as a stick, and told her to come back in ten years for a real one. She came back in nine. She's a knight. She's the one who knighted me, in the end. A strange world."
golden_sword | For the Prince Who Wanted to Look Good | "He wanted gold, with jewels. I told him gold makes a terrible sword. He said that wasn't the point. I made it. It was beautiful and useless. He wore it at every ceremony for forty years and never once drew it. Best customer I ever had. Never once needed me."
stone_sword | For the Farmer Who Couldn't Pay | "Stone, because he couldn't afford iron. He needed it for wolves. I made it as well as I've ever made anything. He paid me in eggs, every week, for eleven years. I didn't like eggs much. I ate every one. It's the only sword I've made that's still in daily use."
iron_sword | For the Old Soldier | "He'd lost his sword in a river, the one he'd carried for thirty years. He wanted the same one. I made it the same, as close as I could. He held it and wept. Then he said, 'it's not the same.' I said, 'no. But you are.' He took it. He came back for a drink every year."
diamond_sword | For the Woman With the Dragon | "She came for a diamond sword, to kill the dragon that took her village. I made it. She came back a year later and laid it on my anvil and said she didn't use it. The dragon had a child. She couldn't. She asked me to melt it down. I didn't. It hangs over my bed."
copper_sword | For Myself | "The last one. Copper, because it was what was left. I'd never made myself a sword in fifty years. I hung it over the forge door. My apprentices touch it for luck on the way in. I don't tell them it isn't lucky. It's just the last one. Perhaps that's lucky enough."

@ sixhoes | The Six Hoes of the Garden Allotments
> On the allotments behind the old mill, six gardeners have tended six plots side by side for thirty years, and between them they have won every vegetable prize in the county, and they have hated each other for every one of those thirty years. The hoes they use are as famous as they are. So are the arguments.
wooden_hoe | Mrs Pemberton's Hoe | Mrs Pemberton has won the marrow prize eleven years running and keeps the trophy on her kitchen table where the vicar can see it when he visits. She uses an old wooden hoe her grandmother left her. She says the wood knows the soil. The soil, she says, knows her.
wooden_hoe | Mr Okafor's Hoe | Mr Okafor grows peppers so hot that the judges have twice had to leave the tent. He carved his hoe himself from a branch of the pear tree in his garden, and talks to it in a language none of the others speak. They're sure he's insulting their leeks. He is. He's told them so.
golden_hoe | The Widow Crabtree's Hoe | The Widow Crabtree's late husband gave her a golden hoe for their fortieth anniversary, as a joke, and she has used nothing else since. It's bent into a shape no hoe should be. Her potatoes win every year. She says the secret is grief and horse manure, in roughly equal measure.
golden_hoe | Mr Fanshawe's Hoe | Mr Fanshawe bought a golden hoe because he read in a magazine it would improve his onions. It did not. Gold is soft. It bent on the first stone. He uses it anyway, out of pride. His onions are the worst on the allotments. He blames the soil, the weather and Mrs Pemberton.
diamond_hoe@ancient_city | The Brigadier's Hoe | The Brigadier came back from somewhere very deep and dark with a diamond hoe, and won't say where he got it. He gardens in a straight line, with military precision, and grows exactly one hundred carrots a year, all the same length. He has never entered a competition. He says it would be unfair.
wooden_hoe | The Newcomer's Hoe | Last spring a young woman took over the sixth plot, empty since old Dennis died, with a cheap hoe and no idea what she was doing. The other five, who hadn't agreed on anything for thirty years, all came over to help her on the first morning. They're friends now. It's very confusing for everyone.
