# Stories, thirtieth book: many of them on the rarer things (spears, copper, chainmail, golden and diamond tools).

@ sevenaxes | The Woodcutter's Seven Axes
> Old Bertram felled trees in the Hollin woods for seventy years, and in that time he owned exactly seven axes, and he kept every one. They hang on the wall of his cottage in a row, from the smallest to the last, and if you ask him about any of them he will tell you what year it was, and who he was then.
wooden_axe | The First Axe | His father carved it for him when he was six, from a branch of the first tree he ever watched fall. It couldn't cut anything. He chopped at the woodpile with it every evening anyway, very seriously, while his father did the real work beside him and pretended not to watch.
stone_axe | The Second Axe | At twelve, a stone head lashed to an ash handle. He felled his first tree with it, a small birch, and it took him the whole day, and when it came down he sat on the stump and cried, and he never could explain why. His father said that was how you knew you'd be good at it.
copper_axe | The Third Axe | Copper, bought with his first wages at seventeen, and bright as a new coin. He polished it more than he used it. A girl from the mill laughed at him for it. He let it go green after that, out of embarrassment. He married the girl. She still laughs about the axe.
iron_axe | The Fourth Axe | The working axe. Forty years of it. Every beam in the village and half the boats on the river came out of the woods on the end of this axe. There's a notch in the handle for every winter. He can tell you what each one was for. His wife says he makes half of them up.
golden_axe | The Fifth Axe | The village gave it to him at sixty, for his long service, made of gold and engraved with every great tree the village could remember him felling. He never once cut with it. He hung it on the wall with great ceremony and went back to the iron one the same afternoon.
diamond_axe | The Sixth Axe | His son found diamonds in the hills and had this made for his father, to make the work lighter. It did. Trees fell like grass. Bertram used it for a month and then put it on the wall, because, he said, a tree should take a bit of effort. It deserves that much.
wooden_axe | The Seventh Axe | The last one, carved by his granddaughter when she was six, for him. It can't cut anything. He chops at the woodpile with it every evening, very seriously, while she does the real work beside him now. He pretends not to watch. She knows he's watching.

@ rattlingregiment | The Rattling Regiment
> The duke's personal guard wore chainmail from head to foot, and marched in perfect step, and could be heard coming from three valleys away. They were the noisiest soldiers in the history of the world. They never once surprised an enemy. They never once lost a battle either, which the historians still can't explain.
chainmail_helmet | The Sergeant's Coif | The sergeant believed that a soldier who rattled was a soldier who was proud. He inspected every coif each morning by shaking it next to his ear. If it didn't rattle enough, he loosened the links. The regiment's hearing was, by the end, very poor.
chainmail_chestplate | The Marching Shirt | Two hundred men in chainmail marching in step make a sound like a waterfall full of cutlery. Enemy armies heard them coming for a day and a half. Usually, by the time the regiment arrived, the enemy had worked themselves into such a state they surrendered.
chainmail_leggings | The Night Raid | The regiment was once ordered to make a silent night raid. They wrapped their legs in blankets. It helped a little. The enemy woke up anyway, and came out to see what the noise was, and the regiment, not knowing what else to do, saluted them. A truce was signed by dawn.
chainmail_boots | The Parade Boots | On parade day, the whole city came out with their hands over their ears to watch the regiment march past. Children threw flowers. Dogs howled. A baker's window cracked. The duke declared it the finest parade in history and doubled their pay.
iron_spear | The Standard-Bearer's Spear | The banner of the regiment flew from this spear in nineteen battles. The standard-bearer was the only man in the regiment who wore leather instead, so that he could hear his orders. He was also the only one who ever got hurt. He says it was worth it.

@ gildedgarden | The Gilded Gardeners of Lord Fennimore
> Lord Fennimore was so rich and so vain that he gave his gardeners golden tools, so that visitors to his famous gardens would see the gold flash in the sun. Golden tools are useless. Gold is too soft. The gardeners had to make his gardens beautiful anyway. This is the story of how they did it, and what they did with the tools.
golden_hoe | The Head Gardener's Hoe | The head gardener tried to hoe the rose beds with it on her first morning and it folded in half on a pebble. She bent it back. It folded again. She hid it in the shed and used her grandmother's wooden one, and carried the golden one about for show when his lordship walked past.
golden_shovel | The Under-Gardener's Shovel | It bent on the first spadeful. The under-gardener dug the whole of the new lake with an old iron shovel at night, by lantern light, and in the mornings he stood beside it holding the golden one, smiling, while Lord Fennimore admired the progress.
golden_axe | The Hedge-Trimmer's Axe | Lord Fennimore wanted hedges shaped like swans. Golden axes cannot shape hedges. The hedge-trimmer used shears borrowed from the village and a great deal of patience. The swans were magnificent. His lordship praised the golden axe. The trimmer bowed.
golden_pickaxe | The Rockery Pick | The rockery took a year. The golden pick took one blow to ruin. The gardeners built the whole thing with tools they bought themselves, out of their own wages. When Lord Fennimore died, the gardeners sold the golden tools, every one, and split the money.
golden_helmet | The Garden Crown | With the money from the tools, the gardeners bought the gardens. They're open to everyone now, free, every day. In the gatehouse there's a golden helmet, the only gold thing left, which the head gardener wears on the first day of spring. She says it was the only gift his lordship ever gave that fit.

@ diamondfarm | The Farmer Who Struck Diamonds
> Ned Fallow was digging a new well in his turnip field when he hit diamonds, a whole seam of them, enough to buy the county. He didn't buy the county. He made himself new farm tools out of them, every single one, and went on growing turnips, very, very efficiently. The county never understood him.
diamond_shovel | The Well Shovel | He kept digging the well, after he found the diamonds, because the farm needed a well. He dug it with a diamond shovel. It took him an afternoon. He says it's the best well in the county. It is. The water tastes faintly of money, according to the neighbours.
diamond_hoe | The Turnip Hoe | His turnips were the straightest in the land, the rows so perfect you could see them from the hill like lines on a page. The diamond hoe never blunted. Ned said a good tool was one you forgot you were holding. He never once forgot. He said that was the problem with diamonds.
diamond_axe | The Fencing Axe | He built his fences with a diamond axe, which meant they were up in a morning, and then he had the rest of the day free, which he hadn't had in forty years. He didn't know what to do with it. He sat on the fence and watched the turnips grow. He said it was lovely.
diamond_pickaxe | The Seam | Every now and then he went down to the seam and took out one more diamond, when somebody in the village needed something: a roof, a doctor, a cow. He never took more. The seam is still there, under the turnips, mostly. Nobody but his grandchildren knows where.
leather_boots | Ned's Old Boots | The only thing on the farm that wasn't diamond, in the end, was Ned. He wore the same old boots until the day he died, patched a hundred times. When the lawyer read his will, there was a line in it for the boots: "to whoever needs boots". The village still passes them on.

@ marooned | The Account of a Marooned Sailor
> Being the true account of Jonah Penrose, ship's boy, set ashore on an island with no name by a mutinous crew, with nothing but his knife and his wits; who lived there four years and built himself a life from stone and wood; as told by himself to the captain of the ship that found him, and set down in his own words.
wooden_pickaxe | The First Tool | The first week I ate shellfish and cried. The second week I found an old tree on the beach and made this, with my knife, out of a branch, and with it I dug into the side of the hill for stone. It broke on the fourth day. I made another. I have made forty. I am good at it now.
stone_axe | The Shelter | With stone I made an axe, and with the axe I cut poles, and with the poles I built a hut against the rock on the windward side. It fell down in the first storm. I built it again on the leeward side. It has stood three years. I learned more from the first one.
stone_pickaxe | The Cave | Behind the hut I dug into the hill and found a cave, and in the cave I found that someone had lived there before me. There were marks on the wall, scratched with a stone. Hundreds of them. They stopped. I added mine below them. I didn't want to think about why they stopped.
stone_spear | The Fishing Spear | I learned to fish with a spear in the shallows, standing very still, the way the herons do. It took me a whole year to catch a fish that way. After that, I never went hungry again. The herons and I fish side by side now. They are better than me. I have told them so.
stone_sword | The Last Morning | On the morning the ship came, I was on the beach with this, which I had made for wild pigs, and I nearly ran into the trees, thinking it was the mutineers come back. It wasn't. It was a captain with a kind face who said "good lord, boy". I am going home. I have written this so I remember.

@ joustinglances | The Last Joust at Harrowmere
> For three hundred years the knights of the eastern march jousted every summer at Harrowmere, riding at each other with long spears across the lists until one of them fell. The last joust ever held there was the strangest. Four knights rode in it. One of them was eleven years old. One was a ghost, possibly.
iron_spear | Sir Aldous's Lance | Sir Aldous had won the Harrowmere joust nine years running and meant to make it ten. He was the best rider in the march, the best spearman, and, everyone agreed, the most unbearable man in it. He lost in the first round, to the eleven-year-old. He has never been the same.
stone_spear | The Page Girl's Lance | The page girl entered herself because a knight had fallen ill, and nobody could find the rule that said she couldn't. She rode a pony. She carried a stone spear she'd made. She aimed for Sir Aldous's shield and hit it squarely and he simply fell off. Nobody understands how.
copper_spear | The Green Knight's Lance | A knight in green armour rode in that nobody knew, and won two rounds without a word, and in the final faced the page girl, and lowered his copper spear before the charge and bowed to her. Then he rode out of the lists and was not seen again. The old men say he rode in once before, a century ago.
diamond_spear | The Prize | The prize was a diamond spear, kept by the lord of Harrowmere for three hundred years. The page girl was given it, and the lord said that was the end of the joust: there was no point holding another, he said, when it had been won so well. She keeps it in her hall. She's a knight now.

@ coppersky | The Copper Airship of Varrow
> In the age of copper, before iron was common, the city of Varrow tried to build a ship that could sail the sky. It was made entirely of copper: its hull, its ribs, its engines, its crew's armour. It flew for eleven minutes. The chronicle of the attempt is the most enthusiastic document in the city archive.
copper_chestplate | The Engineer's Plate | The engineer, a woman named Isolde Varr, wore copper armour that she'd built herself, with pockets for tools, a pocket for tea, and a small lightning rod on the back, which she said was "probably unnecessary". The chronicle notes that it was struck by lightning twice during the flight.
copper_leggings | The Crew's Greaves | The crew of eleven wore copper from head to foot, so that, the engineer explained, the ship would "know" them. The chronicle records that the crew did not understand this and neither does the chronicler, but that the ship did seem to fly better with them aboard.
copper_boots | The Boots on the Deck | Copper boots on a copper deck in the sky make a sound like a bell choir. The chronicle reports that the whole city below heard them walking about, eleven minutes of it, a ringing in the clouds. Children in Varrow still play a game called "the sky bells".
copper_axe | The Mooring Axe | When the ship began to come down, rather faster than intended, the engineer cut the mooring cables with this axe so the ship wouldn't drag the tower down with it. It landed in the lake. Everyone swam to shore. The chronicle calls this "a most successful landing".
copper_sword | The Engineer's Toast | At the celebration feast that night, the engineer raised this copper sword, which had been part of the ship's rigging, and announced she would build a bigger one. The chronicle ends: "The city council has forbidden it. She is building it anyway, in her barn. We all hope it flies."

@ shovelbrigade | The Snow Shovel Brigade of High Kettle
> High Kettle is the highest village in the mountains, and every winter the snow buries it to the rooftops. The village keeps a brigade of snow-shovellers, ranked by the shovel they've earned, who dig it out each morning. It is the most respected job in the village. It is also the coldest. Applicants are warned.
iron_shovel | The Recruit's Shovel | Every recruit gets an iron shovel and a speech from the captain about the noble war against the snow. Then they dig the path to the well, which takes four hours, and come back unable to feel their faces. Half of them quit on the first day. The other half are lifers.
golden_shovel | The Ceremonial Shovel | Once a year, the brigade elects the Shoveller of the Year, who carries the golden shovel at the head of the parade. It's useless for snow, and everyone knows it. The Shoveller carries it anyway, very proudly, while the rest of the brigade digs a path for the parade in front of them.
diamond_shovel | The Captain's Shovel | Only the captain carries a diamond shovel. She earned it in the Great Drift, the winter the snow reached the church spire and she dug the whole village out alone in three days, singing. The village still sings her song when it snows. She hates the song. She's eighty.
leather_boots | The Frozen Boots | Brigade boots are stuffed with straw, wrapped in wool, greased with goose fat and still freeze solid by noon. Shovellers stand them by the fire at night in a long row. The row of boots by the brigade hall fire is the first thing visitors to High Kettle are shown. It's very long.

@ diamondwedding | Sixty Years of Diamonds
> On their wedding day, Elsbeth gave Harald a diamond, because he'd always said he wanted to be a knight. Every twenty years after that, she gave him another piece of diamond armour, made from what she'd saved. He never went to war. He wore the armour to the village fair every summer. This is the set, and the years.
diamond_helmet | The Wedding Gift | The first year, she couldn't afford a whole helmet, so she had the diamond set into an old iron one. He wore it to the wedding feast and danced in it. It fell off into the soup. He wore it again the next day, still smelling of soup. She says that's when she knew she'd chosen right.
diamond_chestplate | The Twentieth Year | For their twentieth, a breastplate, saved for coin by coin from her eggs, her cheese and her lace. He cried when he opened it. Then he wore it to plough the field, which she said was ridiculous. He said a knight should be useful. The field was ploughed beautifully.
diamond_leggings | The Fortieth Year | For their fortieth, greaves. By then the whole village knew about the armour and helped her save, without telling him. He wore the whole set to the fair that summer, clanking, beaming, and the children followed him round all day calling him Sir Harald. He knighted all of them.
diamond_boots | The Sixtieth Year | For their sixtieth, the boots, the last piece. He was too frail to stand in the whole set by then, so she helped him put the boots on, and he sat by the window in them, and watched the fair from there. He said: "I am the richest knight in the world." He was.

@ hoedown | The Great Hoe-Down
> Every harvest the farming families of the valley hold a dance in the big barn called the Hoe-Down, and every year the farmer with the best harvest gets to call the steps. The steps are always the same. The jokes are always the same. The jokes are always terrible. Nobody would have it any other way.
wooden_hoe | The Caller's Hoe | The caller raises a wooden hoe to start the dance and says the same thing every year: "Grab your partner, grab your hoe, it's time to let your feelings grow." The whole barn groans. The fiddle starts. Everyone dances. Nobody has ever explained what feelings have to do with turnips.
golden_hoe | The Prize Hoe | The best harvest of the year wins the golden hoe, which nobody would ever use in a field, which is the point. "It's a hoe-nour," says the caller, handing it over. The barn groans. The winner says, "I'm hoe-verwhelmed." The barn groans louder. It's tradition.
diamond_hoe | The Legendary Hoe | Old Mother Garrow won the golden hoe twenty years in a row, and the valley gave her a diamond one instead, in a glass case. She calls it "my hoe-ly relic". Every year she brings it to the barn, sets it on a chair and says "a hoe for every season". Nobody knows what it means.
leather_boots | The Dancing Boots | Boots that have danced every Hoe-Down for thirty years, resoled eleven times, belonging to the farmer's wife who's never once missed a step. Her husband trod on her toes in the first dance of their first Hoe-Down. She married him anyway. He says he swept her off her feet.

@ banishedknights | The Banished Knights of the Chain Order
> When the Chain Order was cast out of the capital for a crime no one now remembers, its knights took their armour and went into the ash wastes, and swore they would return when the crown was cleansed. The crown was never cleansed. They wander the wastes still, in chainmail gone black, and they do not speak of why.
chainmail_helmet | The Coif of the Exile | Each banished knight wears a coif of chain that has not been removed since the day they were cast out. Their faces have grown into it. They speak through it, slowly, as if remembering how. Ask one his name and he will tell you the name of the order. That is all that is left.
chainmail_chestplate | The Hauberk of the Long Oath | Black with ash, heavy with the dust of a hundred years. The oath is etched on the inside, link by link, in letters too small to read without taking it off. None of them will take it off. Some say the oath is a lie. Some say it is a confession.
iron_spear | The Spear of the Order | They fight with spears, in a line, as they were taught, though there are only a few left and the line is very short. They will not break it. A traveller who watched them fight wolves said the knights did not move until the wolves were close enough to touch, and then the wolves were gone.
chainmail_boots | The Steps in the Ash | Their tracks in the ash wastes never cross. They walk in long circles round the capital, at a distance, as if looking for a way back in. Every year the circles are a little smaller. In a hundred years, the scholars say, they will reach the gate.

@ mystonesword | My Stone Sword
> When I was eight, Dad said I could make my first real tools, out of stone, like he did when he was little. I made a sword, a pickaxe and an axe. They're not very good. I still have them. Mum says I can put them in a story if I'm quick because tea is in ten minutes.
stone_sword | My Sword | My sword is made of stone and a stick and it's a bit wonky because the stone was wonky. Dad says all the best swords have a bit of character. I named it Grumble because it makes a grumbly noise when you swing it. I fought a zombie with it once. Well, I fought near a zombie.
stone_pickaxe | My Pickaxe | I mined with my pickaxe for a whole afternoon and I found coal and some more stone and a bone. Dad said the bone was from a skeleton. I said it was from a dinosaur. We agreed it was from a skeleton dinosaur. I keep it on my shelf. It's my best find.
stone_axe | My Axe | I chopped a tree down with my axe. It took ages and my arms hurt and the tree fell the wrong way and landed on Dad's shed. Dad said "well, it needed a new roof anyway" and we built a new roof together. It's a much better roof. I chose the colour. It's red.
wooden_pickaxe | Dad's Old Pickaxe | Dad gave me his first pickaxe, the wooden one he made when he was eight, which Grandma kept in a box all this time. It's even wonkier than mine. Dad says Grandma used to say his tools had character too. I think having character runs in the family.

@ goldenduke | The Duke Who Wore Gold Trousers
> The Duke of Glittershire wore golden armour every day of his life: golden helm, golden plate, golden greaves, golden boots. He said it was so his people could always see him. They could. He could be seen from the other side of the valley. He could not, however, run, swim or sit down comfortably, and gold is not much use in a fight.
golden_helmet | The Duke's Helm | Polished every morning by a servant whose only job was polishing the helm. On sunny days, it was so bright that birds flew into it. The duke said this proved his radiance. His physician said it proved he should wear a hat.
golden_chestplate | The Duke's Plate | He went to war in it once. The enemy archers saw him coming from four miles off and aimed at him all day. Gold, it turns out, stops arrows rather well, at least for a while. He came back covered in them, like a hedgehog, delighted. He said they were tributes.
golden_leggings | The Duke's Greaves | The trousers, as everybody called them. He was very proud of the trousers. He could not bend his knees in them. He had to be lifted onto his horse by four men with a winch. The winch is still in the castle courtyard. It is called the Duke's Ladder.
golden_boots | The Duke's Boots | He fell in the castle moat in these, in his full gold, and sank straight to the bottom, and was pulled out by a stable boy who dived in after him. The duke made the boy a knight on the spot, dripping, and then gave him the boots. The boy sold them and bought a farm.
